Concept 01 — The physical layer

Layer 1 doesn't care what the data says.

It takes finished frames from Layer 2, turns them into signals the medium can carry, and pushes them out. Content is irrelevant. Only signal integrity matters.

Physical hardware, encoding schemes, signalling methods. Three things, and the standards cover all of them.

The physical layer receives fully formed frames from the data link layer, encodes them into signals appropriate for the medium, and delivers them. At the other end they're decoded and passed back up.

Physical hardware — NICs, cables, connectors, transmission interfaces. Ethernet NICs for wired, WLAN NICs for wireless.

Encoding converts digital bits into patterns the receiver can recognise and decode.

Signalling determines how those patterns physically travel — and that depends entirely on the medium you picked.

Worth pausing on: what does copper actually send down the wire? Not ones and zeros. Electrical pulses.

Concept 02 — Performance metrics

Promised, delivered, useful.

Bandwidth is what your ISP promises. Throughput is what you usually get. Goodput is what your download actually uses. Three numbers, and they get smaller in that order.

Bandwidth is theoretical. Throughput is real. Goodput is real and useful.

Bandwidth — the theoretical maximum capacity of a link, in kbps, Mbps or Gbps. Under ideal conditions. It excludes nothing, because it's a limit, not a measurement.

Throughput — the actual rate achieved right now. Lower than bandwidth because of congestion, latency and hardware limits. It still counts overheads and retransmissions.

Goodput — useful data delivered to the application, with protocol overhead and retransmissions stripped out. The smallest of the three.

Latency is different in kind: not a rate at all, but the delay from source to destination.

The module's analogy: bandwidth is how many lanes the highway has. Throughput is how many cars are actually moving in rush hour. Goodput counts only the passengers who reached their destination.

Concept 03 — Copper cabling

Cheap, easy, and easily disturbed.

Copper is affordable and simple to deploy. It also weakens over distance, picks up interference from anything electrical nearby, and leaks between its own wire pairs.

Attenuation, EMI/RFI, crosstalk. Three problems, and the cable types exist to fight them.

Attenuation — the signal weakens over distance.

EMI and RFI — electromagnetic and radio frequency interference from nearby electrical equipment.

Crosstalk — signal leaking between adjacent wires inside the same cable.

UTP is the most common Ethernet cable. The twisting itself reduces crosstalk through signal cancellation — that's what the twists are for. STP adds shielding for electrically noisy environments. Coaxial uses a central conductor with shielding, common in older networks and antenna connections.

Then there's which cable to grab: straight-through connects different device types, like a switch to a PC. Crossover connects similar devices directly, like switch to switch.

Concept 04 — Fibre and wireless

Light goes furthest. Radio goes anywhere.

Fibre carries light through ultra-pure glass and is completely immune to interference. Wireless trades all physical constraints for coverage limits, interference and security risk.

Fibre is immune to EMI and RFI. Copper never is. That's the headline difference.

Fibre optic transmits data as light through strands of ultra-pure glass or plastic, with minimal attenuation over long distances. Classified by mode: single-mode or multi-mode, each optimised for different distance and bandwidth needs.

Where you find it: enterprise backbone links, fibre-to-the-home, submarine cable systems, and long-haul provider networks connecting cities and countries.

Wireless sends data as electromagnetic signals — radio and microwave. It costs you coverage (obstacles block it), interference resistance, and security, since signals can be intercepted without physical access.

Most wireless operates half-duplex — transmit or receive, never both at once. That halves the effective bandwidth per device.

The IEEE standards worth knowing: 802.11 Wi-Fi, 802.15 Bluetooth, 802.16 WiMAX, 802.15.4 Zigbee.

Concept 05 — The data link layer

Two sublayers, and they don't do the same job.

Layer 2 hides the physical medium from everything above it. LLC manages the conversation. MAC does the packaging, the addressing and the error checking.

LLC manages communication and flow control. MAC handles encapsulation, delimiting, addressing and error detection.

The data link layer enables NICs to communicate directly with each other, whatever the medium underneath.

Its key trick is abstraction — hiding whether the data is crossing copper, fibre or air, so that IP at Layer 3 never has to care. That's what lets upper layers work identically on any medium.

Logical Link Control (LLC) manages communication and flow control between devices.

Media Access Control (MAC) handles data encapsulation, frame delimiting, addressing and error detection.

Concept 06 — Topologies, duplex, access

Shape of the wiring, shape of the conversation.

Physical topology is where the cables actually run. Logical topology is how frames move and how devices take turns. WANs and LANs favour different shapes.

WAN: point-to-point, hub-and-spoke, mesh. LAN: star, extended star, bus, ring.

Physical topologies define the actual layout of cabling and connections. Logical topologies influence how frames are structured and how media access control operates.

Half-duplex — one device transmits at a time, alternating send and receive. Full-duplex — both directions at once, improving throughput.

A duplex mismatch, where one end is full and the other half, causes collisions, latency and degraded throughput. It's a configuration error, not a hardware fault.

On shared media, access has to be coordinated. Contention-based access has devices listen before transmitting — CSMA/CA in WLANs, operating half-duplex. Controlled access assigns specific times or tokens, which is more orderly and predictable.

Switch-based Ethernet sidesteps the whole problem by supporting full-duplex.

Concept 07 — The Ethernet frame

Header, payload, trailer. And a size limit at both ends.

Layer 2 wraps the Layer 3 packet in a frame. Too small and it's a runt. Too big and it's a jumbo. Either way the receiver drops it.

64 bytes minimum. 1518 bytes maximum. Outside that range, discarded.

Encapsulation at Layer 2 takes an IPv4 or IPv6 packet from the network layer and wraps it in a frame with three parts: a header carrying addressing and control fields, the payload — the original Layer 3 packet — and a trailer holding error detection data, the Frame Check Sequence.

Frame fields vary by medium and topology, but typically include start and stop delimiters, MAC addresses, type and control indicators, the data itself, and error detection codes.

Ethernet enforces size boundaries. Below 64 bytes a frame is a collision fragment or runt. Above 1518 it's a jumbo or baby giant. Receiving devices discard both.

Concept 08 — MAC addressing

48 bits, burned in at the factory, half of it not the vendor's choice.

A MAC address identifies a device on the local segment. The first 24 bits are registered with the IEEE. The last 24 are the vendor's to assign.

First 24 bits: the OUI, registered with the IEEE. Last 24 bits: assigned by the vendor.

MAC addresses are 48-bit identifiers written in hexadecimal, base 16, because it's compact and readable. Binary 0000 1011 becomes 0B.

Vendors must register with the IEEE for a 24-bit Organisationally Unique Identifier. The remaining 24 bits are theirs. The whole thing is embedded in the NIC at manufacture — the Burned-In Address — though some systems allow a temporary software override.

Every frame carries a source and destination MAC. The source is always unicast. The receiving NIC checks the destination: if it matches its own address, or a recognised broadcast or multicast address, the frame is accepted and passed up. Otherwise it's dropped.

To find the MAC behind an IP, devices use ARP for IPv4 and Neighbor Discovery for IPv6.

Concept 09 — Hop by hop

The MAC addresses change at every router. The IP addresses never do.

This is the single most tested idea in the module. A frame is local. It gets torn down and rebuilt at every segment boundary. The packet inside survives the whole journey unchanged.

MAC addresses are only meaningful within the local segment. Routing between networks uses hierarchical IP addresses.

The data link layer's scope stops at the segment. For anything crossing networks, Layer 3 takes over.

A router receives a frame and de-encapsulates it to reach the IP packet inside. It reads the destination IP, works out the optimal path, then re-encapsulates that same packet in a brand new frame built for the next segment.

New frame means new MAC addresses: the router's own NIC as source, the next device's NIC as destination.

Concept 10 — Ethernet switching

A switch starts out knowing nothing.

The MAC address table isn't configured, it's learned. Every frame that arrives teaches the switch where the sender lives. Until it knows the destination, it floods.

Learn the source. Look up the destination. Flood if it isn't there yet.

Switches operate at Layer 2 and use a MAC address table — also called a CAM table — to pick the right output port for each frame.

They typically use store-and-forward switching: buffer the entire frame, verify it with the FCS, and only then forward it. Invalid frames never leave.

Buffering handles congestion two ways. Port-based reserves memory per port. Shared memory pools it and allocates dynamically based on traffic.

Auto negotiation works out the best speed and duplex for each link. Auto-MDIX detects whether the cable is straight-through or crossover and configures the port to match — which is why you can plug almost anything into anything now.
