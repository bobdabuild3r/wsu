Concept 03 — Masks and CIDR

Three ways to write the same thing.

Binary, dotted decimal, and slash notation all describe one boundary. Being able to move between them on sight is most of what subnetting asks of you.

/24 and 255.255.255.0 and 11111111.11111111.11111111.00000000 are the same statement.

CIDR — Classless Inter-Domain Routing — is the compact form. Also called prefix length or slash notation. The number after the slash is how many bits belong to the network.

192.168.1.0/24 means the first 24 bits are the network and the remaining 8 are for hosts. That's the same as the mask 255.255.255.0.

The octet values a mask can take are fixed, because the 1s must be consecutive: 0, 128, 192, 224, 240, 248, 252, 254, 255. Anything else is not a valid mask.

Network bits plus host bits always equals 32. If you know one, you know the other.

Concept 04 — Finding the network address

AND the address with the mask. That's the whole trick.

A bitwise AND keeps a bit only where both inputs are 1. Run it across an address and its mask and the host bits vanish, leaving the network address behind.

1 AND 1 gives 1. Everything else gives 0.

The network address is the bitwise AND of the IP address and the subnet mask. It identifies the starting address of the subnet the host belongs to.

It works because the mask is 1s over the network bits and 0s over the host bits. ANDing keeps every network bit exactly as it was and forces every host bit to zero.

Take the module's own example. 192.168.5.130 with mask 255.255.255.192. Only the last octet does anything interesting: 10000010 AND 11000000 gives 10000000, which is 128.

Network address: 192.168.5.128. The host was .130, but the subnet starts at .128.

Same network address means talk directly. Different means find a router.

Before sending anything, a device ANDs its own address and the destination with the same mask, then compares. That single comparison decides whether a router is involved at all.

    Concept 06 — Unicast, broadcast, multicast

    One device, every device, or a group that opted in.

    Three delivery models, and broadcast splits into two kinds that behave very differently at a router.

    Limited broadcast stops at the router. Directed broadcast can arrive from outside.

    Unicast identifies a single device, routable across the whole internet. Standard one-to-one traffic.

    Limited broadcast is 255.255.255.255 — all hosts on the sender's own subnet. Used when the sender doesn't yet know the network configuration, like at boot-up or during a DHCP request. Routers never forward it.

    Directed broadcast is the highest address in a specific subnet — in 192.168.100.0/24 that's 192.168.100.255. It reaches every device in that subnet, and the sender can be outside it. Useful for pushing updates, but often disabled because it can be exploited in denial-of-service attacks.

    Multicast reaches a group of devices that joined it, conserving bandwidth. The IPv4 multicast range is 224.0.0.0 to 239.255.255.255.

    Concept 07 — Public, private, special

    Some addresses can cross the internet. Most of yours can't.

    Public addresses are globally unique and managed by IANA. Private ranges are reserved for internal use and stop at the edge. Two more ranges never go anywhere at all.

    10, 172.16–172.31, and 192.168. Learn those three and you can spot a private address instantly.

    Public addresses are globally unique, allocated by IANA through regional registries. 8.8.8.8 is one. Reachable from anywhere, firewalls permitting.

    Private addresses, defined by RFC 1918, are not routable on the public internet. Three ranges: 10.0.0.0–10.255.255.255 (a /8), 172.16.0.0–172.31.255.255 (a /12), and 192.168.0.0–192.168.255.255 (a /16). They reach the internet through NAT.

    Loopback is the 127.0.0.0/8 block, usually 127.0.0.1. Traffic never leaves the device — it tests the local TCP/IP stack.

    APIPA, also called link-local, is 169.254.0.1–169.254.255.254 with mask 255.255.0.0. A device assigns itself one when it can't reach a DHCP server. Seeing 169.254 on a machine tells you DHCP failed.

    Concept 08 — Legacy classful addressing

    The old system that wasted most of the internet.

    Before CIDR, address blocks came in three fixed sizes. You took whichever fit worst. Classful addressing is obsolete now, but it still shows up in exam questions.

    A, B and C for hosts. D for multicast. E for experiments.

    Classful addressing divided the space into five classes based on the leading bits of the first octet.

    Class A for very large networks — ISPs and major corporations. Class B for medium ones like universities. Class C for small businesses and home networks. Class D is reserved for multicast, not device addressing. Class E is reserved for experimental use.

    The problem was waste. An organisation needing 300 addresses had to take a Class B with 65,534, while others couldn't get enough. Fixed sizes fit almost nobody.

    CIDR arrived in the 1990s and let the boundary sit anywhere. Classful addressing is obsolete, replaced by classless routing and subnetting.

    Concept 09 — Broadcast domains

    A broadcast domain ends at a router. Never at a switch.

    Every device in a broadcast domain has to process every broadcast, whether it cares or not. Grow the domain and that cost grows with it. Segmentation is the fix.

    Switches extend a broadcast domain. Routers break it up.

    A broadcast domain is a logical area where all devices receive each other's broadcasts at Layer 2. It covers everything on the same switched segment — and stops dead at a router, because routers don't forward broadcast traffic.

    Large domains cause real problems. Excessive broadcast traffic — ARP requests and DHCP discoveries pile up, and every device burns CPU processing them. Enough of it is a broadcast storm. Congestion, security risk since ARP spoofing and malware spread more easily when everything can see everything, troubleshooting difficulty, and poor scalability.

    Segmentation solves all of it at once. Three ways to divide: by location — floors, buildings, branches. By function — accounting, sales, guests. By device type — servers, printers, IoT. IoT is the classic case, since weak security belongs on its own segment.

    Concept 10 — Subnetting a /24

    Borrow bits from the hosts. Pay for it in host count.

    Every bit you take from the host portion doubles your subnet count and halves your hosts per subnet. Two powers of two, moving in opposite directions.

    Subnets = 2 to the power of borrowed bits. Usable hosts = 2 to the power of remaining host bits, minus 2.

    A /24 has 8 host bits: 2⁸ = 256 total addresses, 254 usable. You always subtract two — the network address at the bottom and the broadcast address at the top can't be assigned to a device.

    Borrow 2 bits and the mask becomes 255.255.255.192, a /26. That's 2² = 4 subnets, each with 6 host bits left: 2⁶ − 2 = 62 usable hosts.

    The trade never changes. More subnets always means fewer hosts in each.

    Concept 11 — VLSM

    Fixed-size subnets waste addresses. VLSM stops that.

    Give a ten-device office a /26 and you've thrown away fifty addresses. Variable Length Subnet Masking sizes each subnet to what it actually needs — allocated largest first.

    Always allocate largest to smallest. Choose the smallest subnet that still meets the requirement.

    Using one size everywhere causes four problems: wasted addresses — a /26 for a department of ten wastes over 80% of it; scalability issues when a subnet outgrows its block; inflexibility; and management overhead from constant reorganising.

    VLSM uses different subnet masks within the same network, so each subnet borrows however many bits it actually needs.
