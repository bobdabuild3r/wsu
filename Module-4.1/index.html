Concept 01 — The administrator's week

Seven days, seven jobs, and none of them are optional.

Build it, address it, secure it, run the services, plan for growth, watch it, tune it. The module frames the whole role as a week of checklists — here they are in one place.

Design and build come first. Everything after that is keeping it alive.

Network administrators design topologies, configure devices, monitor performance and mitigate threats. The module's framing is deliberately light, but the seven areas map onto a real job description.

Notice the shape: the first two days are building, the middle three are operating, and the last two are improving. Most incidents in the earlier modules trace back to something skipped on one of these lists.

Documentation appears on two separate days. That's not an accident — it's the task most often postponed and most missed during an outage.

    Concept 02 — Choosing the gear

    Fixed hardware is cheaper today and a problem in three years.

    Four buying decisions, and the one the module tests is fixed versus modular. Buying for today's headcount is the mistake that costs most.

    Modular gear can be upgraded. Fixed gear has to be replaced.

    Pick the hardware. Firewalls to keep attackers out, routers to guide traffic, switches to move it internally, access points to serve wireless users. More users means more ports and more capacity.

    Budget properly. Enough ports for every device plus spares, adequate switching capacity and backplane speed, and features like access control and centralised management.

    Check the operating system. NAT, DHCP and DNS support, built-in security such as encryption and firewall rules, QoS settings, and VoIP support if you need it.

    Plan to grow. Fixed devices are predictable but not future-proof. Modular gear can be upgraded, which means less waste and more options. Future-proof with support for 10 Gbps and above, stacking or uplink flexibility, and wireless scalability.

    Concept 03 — ping

    It tells you something is reachable. It never tells you where the break is.

    An ICMP Echo Request out, an Echo Reply back, and a round-trip time. That is the entire tool — and its limits are exactly why traceroute exists.

    ping verifies Layer 3 reachability. Nothing above it, nothing more specific than reachable or not.

    ping sends an ICMP Echo Request to a destination and listens for an Echo Reply. It measures latency as round-trip time, and packet loss.

    Standard ping uses the device's default interface as the source. Extended ping on Cisco IOS lets you change the source address, packet size and repeat count — type ping alone in privileged EXEC mode to get it.

    As a security tool it detects denial-of-service symptoms such as ICMP floods, showing up as unusually high response times or lost packets. It also tests firewall rules by confirming whether ICMP is blocked or allowed as intended.

    Use it to verify only authorised ranges can reach key infrastructure, and check latency regularly to spot scanning or degradation from malicious traffic.

    Concept 04 — traceroute

    It maps the path by deliberately letting packets die.

    Start the TTL at 1 so the first router kills it and complains. Then 2, then 3. Every complaint names a hop. Three asterisks mean somebody chose not to answer.

    Each router that decrements TTL to zero returns ICMP Time Exceeded — and that reply is the hop.

    The packet's TTL starts at 1 and increases by one each iteration. Every router that forwards it decrements TTL by 1. When TTL hits zero the router drops the packet and sends back ICMP Time Exceeded. The process repeats until the destination answers or a hop goes silent.

    The implementations differ. Cisco IOS traceroute uses UDP packets with invalid ports, and the destination replies ICMP Port Unreachable. Windows tracert uses ICMP Echo Requests. Extended traceroute lets you change source IP, maximum TTL and timeouts.

    Hops that don't respond appear as * * *. That may mean a firewall block or a router configured not to answer ICMP — not necessarily a fault.

    For security it reveals intermediary devices that may be intercepting or dropping traffic, and unexpected routing paths that could indicate misconfiguration, black holes or rogue gateways.

    Concept 05 — Host configuration

    Four switches on one command, and each answers a different question.

    ipconfig on its own is almost useless for troubleshooting. The switches are where the diagnosis lives — and one of them exposes DNS poisoning.

    A 169.254 address means DHCP failed. That one reading tells you where to look next.

    Windows. ipconfig shows IPv4 and IPv6 address, subnet mask and default gateway. ipconfig /all adds DNS servers, MAC address and DHCP lease information. ipconfig /release and /renew drop and re-request the lease, which resolves IP conflicts and stale settings. ipconfig /displaydns shows cached DNS entries.

    Linux. ifconfig is deprecated on many systems; ip address or ip a is the modern replacement, with more detail and support for multiple IPs per interface.

    macOS. ifconfig works as on Linux, and networksetup -listallnetworkservices lists every interface.

    The security value: verify the configuration hasn't been manipulated by malware such as rogue DNS settings, spot unauthorised static IPs, examine /displaydns for poisoning, and check /all for a rogue DHCP server handing out false configuration.

    Concept 06 — ARP inspection

    Two IP addresses sharing one MAC is not a coincidence.

    arp -a lists what your machine believes about its neighbours. Read it carefully and ARP spoofing is visible in a single line.

    If the gateway's MAC changed and nothing else did, treat it as an attack until proven otherwise.

    ARP maps an IP address to a MAC address, and it's essential for communication on the local network. arp -a lists every IP-to-MAC pair in the host's cache.

    ARP is a Layer 2 protocol. If IP connectivity fails despite correct IP settings, ARP resolution may be the cause. If a device doesn't appear in the cache, ping it first to trigger resolution.

    For security this is how you identify ARP spoofing — an attacker mapping their own MAC to another device's IP, usually the gateway. It validates MAC-to-IP bindings and detects impersonation.

    The defensive habit: inspect ARP tables regularly and confirm the gateway's MAC hasn't changed unexpectedly. Knowing the attack exists is one thing; this is how you catch it in progress.

    Concept 07 — CDP

    A troubleshooting gift that is also a free map for attackers.

    Cisco Discovery Protocol tells you what's plugged in where, even before IP works. It tells anyone else the same thing, which is why it belongs off on user-facing ports.

    Useful internally. Disabled on anything a user can reach.

    CDP is a proprietary Layer 2 protocol that lets Cisco devices discover directly connected Cisco neighbours — even when Layer 3 IP connectivity isn't established yet. That's what makes it valuable when nothing is working.

    show cdp neighbors lists directly connected Cisco devices. show cdp neighbors detail adds platform, software version, IP address and interface.

    That detail is the problem. An attacker gathers device types, software versions and topology, then targets known vulnerabilities in those exact versions.

    Disable it with no cdp enable per interface, or no cdp run globally.

    The balance: use CDP internally for legitimate discovery and to validate authorised connections, and turn it off on external or user-facing ports.

    Concept 08 — IOS verification

    Eight show commands, each with a second job.

    Every one of these answers an operational question and a security question at the same time. The security column is the half students skip.

    show running-config is an audit tool, not just a config dump.

    These commands give deep insight into router and switch configuration and activity. The module pairs each with its security application, and that pairing is the point of the section.

    show running-config shows active settings — and lets you audit for weak passwords, missing access control lists and open ports.

    show version gives IOS and hardware information — and tells you whether the platform is still supported and patched.

    show ip route displays the routing table — and reveals rogue routes or routing anomalies.

    show interfaces gives physical and logical statistics — and detects flooding, errors, or unused ports left open.

    Concept 09 — Baselines

    You cannot call it abnormal if you never measured normal.

    A baseline is a performance profile gathered over time. Without one, a latency spike is just a number — with one, it is evidence.

    Latency, packet loss, throughput and error rates, logged with timestamps, across different times of day.

    A network baseline defines what normal looks like under different loads. It helps detect anomalies such as sudden latency spikes or throughput drops, supports planning for upgrades and load balancing, and speeds up incident response.

    To establish one, regularly log ping, traceroute, show interfaces and show ip route output, and store the results with timestamps.

    Abnormal spikes in latency or dropped packets can indicate DDoS attacks or botnet traffic.

    Compare new output against the baseline to detect anomalies. Use SNMP-based monitoring, Syslog or NetFlow for automated tracking and alerts, and watch for sudden changes in interface statistics or new routing entries.

    Concept 10 — The six-step process

    Identify, theorise, test, plan, verify, document.

    A structured approach fixes problems faster and finds vulnerabilities on the way through. Each step has its own commands and its own security insight.

    Step 1 is always identify the problem. Step 6 is always document — and it is the one people skip.

    Network problems come from misconfiguration, hardware failure, software error or malicious activity. Working the steps in order stops you fixing symptoms and missing causes.
