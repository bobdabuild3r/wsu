Concept 01 — Network components

Some devices talk. Others carry the talking.

End devices are where data starts and stops. Intermediary devices move it in between. Media is what it travels on. Every network diagram is those three things and nothing else.

End devices are the source and destination. Intermediary devices are everything in the middle.

End devices — computers, smartphones, printers. They interact directly with users, and they initiate or receive the data. Each one has an IP address, the way a house has a street address.

Intermediary devices — routers and switches. They don't create the data, they handle it: routing it to the right destination, regenerating signals, prioritising traffic, detecting errors, enforcing security.

Network media — copper cable, fibre optic, radio waves. The physical or wireless channel the data actually crosses.

Also worth naming: the NIC is the card that lets an end device join the network at all. Ports are the sockets. Interfaces are the specialised ports on routers that act as gateways between separate networks.

Concept 02 — Network scale

LAN, WAN, and everything between.

Networks are classified by scale and purpose. A LAN covers one restricted area. A WAN links LANs across geography. The internet is the largest WAN there is.

LAN is one place. WAN links places. The internet is the biggest WAN.

A LAN operates within a restricted area — a home, an office, a campus. Fast, efficient connections inside a limited space.

A WAN covers large geographic areas, linking multiple LANs through telecommunications infrastructure such as fibre optics or satellites.

Between those two extremes sit the labels the module uses: SOHO for homes and small businesses, enterprise for large organisations with secure access and centralised management, and the internet — a global network made of many smaller public and private networks.

Getting on the internet needs an ISP, offering broadband cable, DSL, wireless WAN or mobile connections.

Concept 03 — Two ways to organise

With a server, or without one.

Client-server puts a specialised machine at the centre. Peer-to-peer removes it and lets devices talk directly. One device can play both roles at once, depending on the software it runs.

In P2P, every device is both client and server. That's the whole idea, and also the whole problem.

Client-server. Clients request services. Servers are specialised computers running software to provide them — storing files, hosting websites, managing email. Centralised, managed, and it scales.

Peer-to-peer. Devices communicate directly with no centralised server, sharing resources like files or printers. Common in homes and small businesses.

P2P wins on simplicity, low cost and ease of setup. It loses on security, scalability, and performance — bottlenecks build as the number of connected devices grows, because nothing is managing the load.

The exam framing: why doesn't a large organisation run peer-to-peer? Because there's no central management, and it doesn't scale.

Concept 04 — Network security

Confidentiality. Integrity. Availability.

Three principles, and every security control in this module serves one of them. If you can sort a scenario into the right bucket, you understand it.

Kept secret. Kept correct. Kept reachable.

Confidentiality — only authorised individuals access sensitive information. Enforced by encryption and access controls.

Integrity — data stays accurate and unchanged in transit. Verified by checksums, hashing and authentication protocols.

Availability — legitimate users can reach services without disruption. Supported by load balancing, redundancy, and defence against attacks.

The threats in the module map onto these: interception and theft breaks confidentiality, tampering breaks integrity, denial of service breaks availability.

Controls scale with the network. Home and small office: antivirus, antispyware, firewall filtering. Corporate: dedicated firewalls, access control lists, intrusion prevention systems, VPNs.

Concept 05 — Transmission types

One, some, or all.

Unicast, multicast and broadcast differ only in how many devices the data is aimed at. Three words, three shapes — easiest marks in the module if you can picture them.

Unicast is one-to-one. Multicast is one-to-a-group. Broadcast is one-to-everyone on the segment.

Unicast sends data directly to a single recipient.

Multicast targets a specific group of devices. It exists to save bandwidth — send once, reach many, without flooding the rest.

Broadcast distributes to every device on the network segment. Every device has to process it whether it cares or not.

The give-away word in a question is group. Group means multicast, every time.

Concept 06 — OSI and TCP/IP

Two models, one job, different resolution.

OSI splits communication into seven layers to explain it. TCP/IP collapses the same work into four because that's what gets built. Your subject teaches OSI — but you're expected to line them up.

Seven layers to think with. Four layers to build with.

OSI is the teaching model: seven layers, each with one responsibility, from raw bits on the wire up to the application a person touches.

TCP/IP is the model that runs the internet. Four layers: network access, internet, transport, application. Same work, coarser divisions.

The three top OSI layers fold into TCP/IP's application layer. The two bottom OSI layers fold into network access. Transport and network map one-to-one.

Concept 07 — Encapsulation

The data doesn't change. Its wrapping does.

Each layer adds its own header on the way down. The receiving device strips them off in reverse. The name of the thing changes at every step — and that name is what gets asked about.

Segment → packet → frame → bits. Four names, one piece of data.

Going down the stack on the sending device, every protocol layer wraps the data in its own header. That's encapsulation.

At the far end the process runs backwards, header by header, until the original data comes back out. That's de-encapsulation.

The form the data takes at each layer is the Protocol Data Unit, or PDU. Packet and frame are the two that get mixed up most often, so learn the order.

Concept 08 — Physical setup

Before you type a single command, you have to get in.

A brand new switch or router has no IP address and no configuration. You can't SSH into something that isn't on the network yet. The console port is how you get in anyway.

Concept 09 — IOS command modes

The prompt tells you where you are.

Every Cisco question starts with reading the prompt. > is user EXEC. # is privileged EXEC. Anything in brackets is a configuration mode. Get that reflex and half the CLI marks are free.

Switch> is limited. Switch# is full power. Switch(config)# is where you change things.

User EXEC, the > prompt, gives limited functionality — basic operations and monitoring.

Privileged EXEC, the # prompt, gives advanced configuration commands and troubleshooting. You get there with enable and back with disable.

Global configuration, Switch(config)#, is where device settings change. Entered with configure terminal.

From there you drop into sub-modes: line console 0 and line vty 0 15 land you in (config-line)#, and interface vlan 1 lands you in (config-if)#.

exit steps back one level. end jumps straight to privileged EXEC from anywhere. That distinction gets tested.

Concept 10 — Config files

RAM forgets. NVRAM remembers.

running-config is live and volatile. startup-config survives a reboot. Losing an hour of lab work almost always comes down to this distinction, not the commands.

If you didn't save it to NVRAM, the reload deletes it.

The running-config sits in RAM. It is the device's current active configuration, and changes take effect the instant you type them.

The startup-config sits in NVRAM. It holds the commands the device executes when it boots.

RAM is volatile. Power off or reload without saving and everything is gone — the device reloads whatever NVRAM was holding.

Concept 11 — Switch Virtual Interface

    
A Layer 2 switch has no IP. Until you give it one.

    
The physical ports on a Layer 2 switch don't do IP addressing — that's Layer 3 work. The SVI is the workaround that makes remote management possible, and interface VLAN 1 is the default.

  

  

    

      
You can't ping a port. You ping the SVI.

      
A Layer 2 switch forwards frames. Its physical ports operate at Layer 2 and don't support Layer 3 IP addressing, so there's nothing on the front of the box to address.

      
Cisco IOS gets around this with a Switch Virtual Interface — a logical interface not tied to any one physical port. It supports both IPv4 and IPv6.

      
The default SVI is interface VLAN 1, the main access point for managing the device across the network.
