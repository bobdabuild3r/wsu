Concept 01 — Threats by impact

Four things an attacker can actually do to you.

Strip away the technique and every network attack ends in one of four outcomes. Naming the outcome is how you work out what it cost.

Steal it, break it, change it, or become you.

Information theft — confidential data such as research or customer records is stolen and misused.

Disruption of service — attacks crash services, hitting availability and productivity. Nothing is stolen; nobody can work.

Data loss and manipulation — the attacker deletes or alters data. Manipulation is the nastier half, because you may not notice.

Identity theft — personal information is used to commit fraud, take out loans or make purchases in someone else's name.

Notice these map onto the CIA triad from Module 1.1. Theft breaks confidentiality, manipulation breaks integrity, disruption breaks availability.

Concept 02 — Technological vulnerabilities

Weaknesses built into the technology itself.

Nobody misconfigured anything. These flaws come with the protocols, the operating systems and the hardware, and you patch or replace rather than reconfigure.

Three sources: the protocols, the operating system, the equipment.

TCP/IP protocol weaknesses. Inherent issues in the core protocols, exploited through spoofing and session hijacking. TCP/IP was designed for a trusted research network, and it shows.

Operating system weaknesses. Outdated or unpatched systems open the door to malware and known exploits. Akira's forgotten Windows Server 2008 with no antivirus and no updates is the textbook case.

Network equipment weaknesses. Unsecured switches and routers become attack vectors themselves rather than just carrying traffic.

The fix is patching, upgrading, decommissioning — not tightening a setting.

Concept 03 — Configuration vulnerabilities

The technology was fine. Somebody set it up badly.

Five configuration failures cover almost every breach in this module. Unlike technological flaws, every one of these is free to fix — it just needs somebody to do it.

Unsecured accounts, guessable passwords, misconfigured services, default settings, misconfigured equipment.

Unsecured user accounts — default or weak passwords make access trivial.

Easily guessed system passwords — predictable credentials are a disaster waiting to happen.

Misconfigured internet services — exposed services and open ports are a common entry point.

Unsecured default settings — leaving factory configuration in place hands attackers the manual.

Misconfigured network equipment — errors that create holes, or block services that should work.

Akira found Telnet enabled with default admin credentials still intact. That single line is three of these at once.

Concept 04 — Policy vulnerabilities

Nothing is broken. Nothing is written down either.

The hardest category to see, because there's no alert for it. Policy vulnerabilities only become visible after something has already gone wrong.

If nobody can find the security policy, there isn't one.

Lack of a written security policy — without formal rules, staff behaviour becomes inconsistent and risky.

Lack of authentication continuity — users authenticated inconsistently across systems, leaving gaps.

Logical access not enforced — if permissions aren't set properly, anyone can reach anything.

Improper software or hardware changes — installing or modifying systems without approval bypasses protections. That's how the forgotten server got onto the network.

No disaster recovery plan — without one, systems can't be restored quickly after an attack.

    Concept 05 — Physical threats

    You can encrypt everything and still lose to a propped-open door.

    Four physical categories, none of which any firewall will help with. Akira hit three of them before lunch.

    Hardware, environmental, electrical, maintenance.

    Hardware threats — theft or tampering. Unauthorised access to network rooms or equipment compromises the entire network. Secure doors, ceilings and vents, use cameras, and log entries electronically.

    Environmental threats — heat and humidity. Equipment fails when the environment isn't controlled. Monitor temperature and humidity continuously.

    Electrical threats — power loss, brownouts and voltage spikes corrupt data or take systems offline. Install a UPS, use power conditioners, and test the backup regularly.

    Maintenance threats — electrostatic discharge, poor labelling, sloppy cabling, careless handling. These cause accidental outages, not malicious ones, and they're the easiest to prevent.

    Note the server room breach: the logs showed no authorised access, yet someone had clearly been in. Absence of a log entry is not absence of an intruder.

    Concept 06 — Malware

    The difference is how it moves, not what it damages.

    Virus, worm and Trojan are classified by their method of spreading. Get that straight and the exam question answers itself.

    A virus needs a host file. A worm needs nothing. A Trojan needs you to invite it in.

    A virus attaches itself to a legitimate program and waits. It activates when someone runs the infected file, then duplicates itself, corrupts data or spies on the user.

    A worm spreads on its own. It exploits vulnerabilities to move between systems with no user action at all, replicating until the network is saturated. One infected device becomes hundreds.

    A Trojan horse is disguised as legitimate software — a free game, a useful tool, a fake update. It requires the user to install and run it. Once opened it creates backdoors, steals passwords, hijacks webcams, or recruits the machine into a botnet.

    Defences are the same three either way: run antivirus, don't open suspicious attachments, and keep software patched so worms find no vulnerability to exploit.

    Concept 07 — Reconnaissance attacks

    Nobody has broken in yet. That's what makes it easy to ignore.

    Four steps, each noisier than the last, and all of them legal-looking. Reconnaissance is the prelude — footprints in the snow outside a locked cabin.

    Public records, then live hosts, then open ports.

    Attackers start with public tools. whois retrieves domain registration details — owner, contacts, IP ranges. nslookup queries DNS to map the address space and find exposed servers and nameservers.

    Then come ping sweeps, sending ICMP echo requests across a range. Every system that replies reveals its position. fping does this far more efficiently than pinging one address at a time.

    Finally port scans. Each live address is probed port by port — like walking a hallway testing every doorknob. An open 80 means a web server, 3389 means remote desktop, and a forgotten FTP server is a gift.

    Every open port tells the attacker what software you run and what might be exploitable.

    Response: monitor and document scanning, block suspicious IPs early, and harden the perimeter with firewalls.

      Concept 08 — Access attacks

      Four ways in, and only one of them involves guessing.

      Password attacks are the obvious route. The other three exploit the relationships between systems rather than the systems themselves — which is why they're harder to spot.

      Guess it, borrow trust, tunnel through, or sit in the middle.

      Password attacks. Brute force hammers the login with thousands of guesses per second. Or the attacker doesn't guess at all — a Trojan logs keystrokes, or a packet sniffer captures unencrypted credentials crossing the network.

      Trust exploitation. Systems inside a network are configured to trust each other. Compromise one and that trust becomes a weapon — the attacker moves sideways through the open gate instead of the front door.

      Port redirection. A compromised machine is used as a launchpad, re-routing attacks through it to reach systems behind the firewall. The traffic appears to come from a trusted host.

      Man-in-the-middle. The attacker sits between two parties, reading and even altering what passes, with neither side aware. Credentials intercepted, sessions injected — invisible unless the connection is properly encrypted.

      Concept 09 — DoS and DDoS

      Not breaking in. Slamming the door shut for everyone else.

      A DoS attack comes from one place and can be blocked. A DDoS arrives from a hijacked army scattered across the world, and blocking them manually is whack-a-mole with no end.

      DoS is one source. DDoS is a botnet.

      DoS floods servers with fake requests — prank callers dialling repeatedly to keep the line busy. Legitimate users are locked out, sites go offline, systems buckle. Nothing is stolen; availability is the target.

      DDoS is the same attack from thousands of sources at once. A botnet of infected machines — laptops, routers, smart TVs, baby monitors — hijacked by malware long ago and activated on command.

      Scale is what makes it dangerous. Traffic arrives from thousands or millions of geographically scattered addresses, so there's no single IP to block.

      Response: activate traffic filtering and rate limiting, use DDoS mitigation services, keep systems patched so your machines don't join a botnet, and coordinate with ISPs to block malicious traffic upstream.

      Eight incidents. Name the category for each.

      This is the whole module in one exercise. Every incident Akira met belongs to one of the four categories you've just covered.

      Technological, configuration, policy, or physical.

      Technological — the flaw is in the protocol, OS or hardware itself. You patch or replace it.
